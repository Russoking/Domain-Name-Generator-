Case Studies

Domain Strategy 4 min read

Real brands, built the same way.

None of these are dictionary words. Each one is two familiar pieces pushed together into something that reads as a single, ownable name — the exact pattern this site's generator automates.

Looking at why these names work is a faster way to develop an eye for a good pairing than reading naming theory. In every case below, the two halves are individually simple — the value comes entirely from how they sit together.

Mailchimp
mail + chimp

A completely mundane first word (mail) paired with an unexpected, playful second word (chimp). The contrast is what makes it memorable — a serious utility with a name that doesn't take itself too seriously, which became the whole personality of the brand.

Basecamp
base + camp

Two words from the same semantic family, which makes the pairing feel inevitable rather than forced. It also does real work as a name: a basecamp is where a team regroups and plans before heading out — an apt metaphor for project management software.

Squarespace
square + space

A geometric word paired with an open, abstract one. Neither word alone says "website builder," but together they evoke clean, structured design — which is exactly the product's pitch.

Dropbox
drop + box

About as literal as a two-word name gets, and that's the point. You drop a file, it goes in a box. The name explains the product's core action without needing a tagline.

Eventbrite
event + brite (bright)

A slightly altered spelling on the second word ("brite" instead of "bright") is a common trick for claiming a name when the literal spelling is taken — while keeping it phonetically identical so it's just as easy to remember and say aloud.

⚠️

A couple of these words appear in unrelated trademarks in other industries — that's usually fine, since trademark protection is category-specific. But before you register anything similar, run a quick search on USPTO.gov (or your country's equivalent) for your specific category, not just the word itself.

Codecademy
code + academy

Two words blended at the seam (academy becomes cademy) rather than simply stacked. It's a slightly more advanced technique than a clean concatenation, and it reads smoothly specifically because "code" and "academy" share that "c-a-d" overlap.

Weight Watchers
weight + watchers

An older example, predating the tech-startup naming wave entirely — proof this pattern isn't a trend, it's a durable naming strategy. Alliteration (the repeated "W" sound) is doing extra work here to make it stick.

Cloudflare
cloud + flare

"Cloud" signals the industry instantly, while "flare" adds motion and energy that a plain infrastructure name would lack. It's a good example of pairing one purely descriptive word with one purely evocative word.

Hootsuite
hoot + suite

An unusual first word (tied to the owl logo) paired with a business-sounding second word ("suite," as in software suite). The mismatch in tone between playful and corporate is deliberate — it makes a fairly dry product category, social media management, feel more approachable.
